# x2

x2 was USRobotics' 56k modem standard from 1997, the rival of K56flex,
before V.90 replaced both. USRobotics' Courier and Sportster modems had
it, and its I-modem and Total Control equipment were the servers. Its
specification was never published.

**D-Modem answers an x2 modem as the server**, on a digital line, with
`AT+MS=X2`. A modem that does not offer x2 gets V.34 instead.

A Courier modem connects to it with error correction and data
compression (`CONNECT .../ARQ/x2/LAPM/V42BIS`) at 32000 bit/s down and
24000 up. Only that first speed is done so far (see
[What is missing](#what-is-missing)).

What D-Modem knows about x2 was learned by watching calls between the
original firmware of a Courier modem and of an I-modem, both running in
an emulator. For the details, see [the technical details](x2-details.md).

## How an x2 call goes

1. V.8, as for V.34. The server marks its V.8 reply as an x2 server's.
2. V.34's line measurements start, and the server's first message is
   marked as an x2 server's. The customer's modem answers with a short
   x2 message instead of measuring the line.
3. The customer's modem sends its V.34 training; the server answers with
   its own digital training: a fixed pattern, two-level training,
   repeated words, the frame alignment, then frames of ones.
4. They exchange their settings in short records with a checksum, and
   the customer's modem ends with V.34's E.
5. In data mode the server sends frames of 6 digital values. At 32000
   bit/s each frame carries 24 bits: 19 choose one of 9 levels for each
   of the 6 values, and 5 are signs. The sixth sign carries no data.
   The customer's modem sends V.34 data back (24000 bit/s).

About 12.7 s into the data the Courier renegotiates its upstream to
28800 bit/s, and in its answer the server switches to the higher speed
the Courier asks for in it (54666 bit/s in the tests; 52000 in some
I-modem calls, which then went to 54666 when the Courier asked again).
The server waits for the Courier to start: if it switched first, the
Courier would drop the call. Like the I-modem, the server sends ones
for about half a second around the switch before the data goes on; the
call then holds at 54666 bit/s.

## What is missing

D-Modem knows two of the higher speeds, 52000 and 54666 bit/s, the
ones the I-modem used; if the Courier asks for another, the call drops.
Only 54666 has been through a call with D-Modem (its 52000 levels are
checked against the I-modem's line octets). A-law lines are not done
yet either.

The sixth sign of each frame carries no data, and the customer's modem
does not check it. During training D-Modem uses the I-modem's own rule
for it, so its training matches the I-modem's octet for octet. In data
mode it keeps the signal balanced around zero, which agrees with the
I-modem's choice about 87% of the time; the Courier behaves the same
either way.

## How it was tested

The Courier's original firmware, in courier-emu's emulator, calls
D-Modem's test program `courier_peer` over a digital line. The Courier
reports `CONNECT 49333/ARQ/x2/LAPM/V42BIS` (its own way of naming the
speed) and prints the text D-Modem sends it. With `courier_peer`
sending a numbered line every second, all of them arrive, before and
after the switch to 54666 bit/s, until the emulator's run ends at about
110 s. The unit test `x2_test`
checks the records against the ones the I-modem and the Courier sent,
and the data frames against a decoder.
