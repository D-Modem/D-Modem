# x2

x2 was the 56k modem standard of USRobotics from 1997, and the competitor of K56flex.
Then V.90 replaced the two standards. The Courier and Sportster modems of USRobotics had
it, and its I-modem and Total Control equipment were the servers. Nobody published its
specification.

**D-Modem answers an x2 modem as the server**, on a digital line, with
`AT+MS=X2`. A modem that does not offer x2 gets V.34 instead.

A Courier modem connects to it with error correction and data
compression (`CONNECT .../ARQ/x2/LAPM/V42BIS`) at 32000 bit/s down and
24000 up. Only that first speed is complete now (refer to
[What is missing](#what-is-missing)).

We learned about x2 from calls between the
original firmware of a Courier modem and of an I-modem. The two operated in
an emulator. For the details, refer to [the technical details](x2-details.md).

## How an x2 call goes

1. V.8, as for V.34. The server marks its V.8 reply as the reply of an x2 server.
2. The line measurements of V.34 start. The server marks its first message
   as the message of an x2 server. The modem of the customer answers with a short
   x2 message, and does not measure the line.
3. The modem of the customer sends its V.34 training. The server answers with
   its digital training: a fixed pattern, two-level training,
   repeated words, the frame alignment, then frames of ones.
4. They exchange their settings in short records with a checksum. The
   modem of the customer sends the E of V.34 at the end.
5. In data mode, the server sends frames of 6 digital values. At 32000
   bit/s, each frame carries 24 bits. 19 bits select one of 9 levels for each
   of the 6 values, and 5 bits are signs. The sixth sign carries no data.
   The modem of the customer sends V.34 data back (24000 bit/s).

At about 12.7 s into the data, the Courier renegotiates its upstream to
28800 bit/s. In this renegotiation, the Courier asks for a higher speed, and in its answer the server
changes to that speed. The speed is 54666 bit/s on a good line, and less on a worse
line (46666, 50666, 52000 or 53333 bit/s). The server changes the speed again when the Courier
asks again later (52000, then 53333, then 54666 on a line 6 dB down).
The server waits for the Courier to start. If the server changed first, the
Courier would drop the call. The server sends ones for about half a second
around the change, as the I-modem does. Then the data continues.

## What is missing

D-Modem knows five of the higher speeds, the speeds that the I-modem used:
46666, 50666, 52000, 53333 and 54666 bit/s. If the Courier asks for
a different speed, the call drops. At 46666, 50666 and 54666, the calls continue and
all lines arrive. At 53333, the Courier accepts none of the
frames of D-Modem, but their levels are the same as the levels of the I-modem. (Then the Courier asked for
54666 and the call continued.) Also, A-law lines are not complete yet.

The sixth sign of each frame carries no data, and the modem of the customer
does not check it. During training, D-Modem uses the rule of the I-modem
for it. Thus, its training is the same as the training of the I-modem, octet for octet. In data
mode, it keeps the signal balanced around zero. This agrees with the
selection of the I-modem about 87% of the time. The Courier operates in the same way
with the two methods.

## How it was tested

The original firmware of the Courier, in the emulator of courier-emu, calls
the test program of D-Modem, `courier_peer`, over a digital line. The Courier
reports `CONNECT 49333/ARQ/x2/LAPM/V42BIS` (its own name for the
speed) and shows the text that D-Modem sends to it. `courier_peer`
sends a numbered line each second. All of these lines arrive, before and
after the change to 54666 bit/s, until the run of the emulator stops at about
110 s. The unit test `x2_test`
compares the records with the records that the I-modem and the Courier sent.
It also checks the data frames against a decoder.
